Tuileries, this twelfth of June 1791

My dear and tender friend,

Your letter reached me on Tuesday, carried through Brussels as you wished, and I have read it three times over. Forgive me for writing at such length: the days here are long, and the nights longer still. I seldom sleep before midnight, and the least noise in the courtyard wakes me. I reach in the dark for a hand that is not there.

The children are well. The Dauphin has a new regiment of lead soldiers and drills them on the carpet with great severity. Madame Royale reads to me in the evening, once the carriage has brought the ladies back from Saint-Cloud. The garden is much changed. The gardener leaves nothing to chance, and has planted lilacs along the wall where the roses were.

I have taken up the harp again. Léonard came on Thursday and found my hair a fright, which I bear with patience, for the word of a hairdresser is the only honest judgement left at this court. The physician orders rest, and I obey him for the sake of the children more than for my own.

Do you remember the road from Vienna, and the inn at Compiègne where the wind blew out every candle? The guard at the gate swore we should be robbed before morning, and we were not. They say the air is good at Montmédy, and better still at Varennes, where my aunt once stopped on her way to Fontainebleau. Perhaps we shall see those places together one day, without a soldier at every door.

I love you madly. Burn nothing of mine. Keep everything, as I keep everything of yours.

M. A.
